# Combining a training-time logic loss with inference-time hard or soft constraints

Scope note: papers were read from their PDF text. Numbers come from their tables. Table values were extracted with `pdftotext`, so check a table against the PDF before quoting it in a formal document. Everything labelled as an inference, including all of the algebra, is mine and is not stated by a cited paper.

## Is there an established method for the two-layer combination, and when does training with constraints beat applying them only at inference?

### Takeaway
There is established precedent and there are names for it: "CL + CI" (constrained learning plus constrained inference), "CLCO" (constrained loss and output), and the L+I, IBT and JIBT family inside Constrained Conditional Models. I found no paper that studies the exact pipeline (per-token categorical semantic loss, a per-token -inf mask, a soft bias and a post hoc verifier, driven by a Horn-rule knowledge base, on a transformer). Across the direct comparisons, the combination is usually best or tied, but its margin over inference-only is usually small. Training helps most with little data, with a weak base model, and when the constraints bring external knowledge. Only inference gives a guarantee.

### Cited Findings

- ROAD-R's inference-only entries are constrained-output models that correct predictions by MaxSAT. The minimal-distance policy (rank 9.50 at 0.5) was worse than the baseline ("detrimental"), while the confidence-weighted AP×O policy (rank 5.42) never did worse than the baseline. The combined best model, CLCO-RCGRU, always satisfies the requirements with f-mAP 31.81 at IoU 0.5, against 30.78 for the baseline RCGRU, whose predictions violate the constraints at least 92% of the time — [ROAD-R](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.01597)
- ROAD-R states that the two uses of constraints "are not mutually exclusive", that constrained loss should give better understanding but no guarantee, and that constrained output is guaranteed but ignores the knowledge in training. Adding a t-norm loss alone gave no significant reduction in the number of violating predictions — [ROAD-R](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.01597)
- Primal-Dual: "constrained learning with constrained decoding consistently performs the best, even though marginal improvements over B+CI are smaller", and the benefit of constrained learning shrinks as training data grows. Caveat: in SRL the Viterbi inference enforces only transition constraints while the training constraints also cover syntactic spans, so violations stay above zero even with inference — [Nandwani et al.](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2019/file/cf708fc1decf0337aded484f8f4519ae-Paper.pdf)
- Wang et al. removed constrained learning and kept only ILP, and F1 fell by 2.6 to 12.3 points. They conclude a well-trained constrained learning model "may not additionally require global inference": ILP added only 0.3 to 0.4 F1 — [Wang et al.](https://aclanthology.org/2020.emnlp-main.51/)
- GLUECons (AAAI 2023) is the broadest benchmark I found. It compares primal-dual (PD), SampL ("an extension of the semantic loss") and ILP, alone and combined, on many tasks. Its conclusions: ILP at inference alone "outperformed other baselines in most of the tasks"; inference has a 0% violation rate by construction while training methods "cannot fully guarantee the consistency"; combining "mainly yields the highest performance on multiple tasks" (NLI at low data: PD+ILP +10.1%, ILP +7.75%), and the authors say the reason needs further investigation — [GLUECons](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.10914)
- GLUECons failure case for inference-only: in semi-supervised arithmetic digit classification the base model scores 9.01%. Adding PD gives +89.39%, adding SampL gives +89.55%, and adding ILP gives -2.11%. "Inference alone cannot help correct the model" when the base model is wildly inaccurate — [GLUECons](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.10914)
- GLUECons violation rates at full data, baseline to +SampL: NLI 9.48% to 5.00%; BeliefBank 0.22% to 0.01%; BIO 2.19% to 0.16%; CausalA 8.60% to 4.37%; Hierarchical Image 39.26% to 36.57%. Simple Image rose from 7.17% to 9.04% — [GLUECons](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.10914)
- GLUECons reports that constraints from external knowledge or structure help most at low data, while self-consistency constraints help less there — [GLUECons](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.10914)
- Sources disagree on whether a training loss lowers violations. Primal-Dual and GLUECons mostly report reductions, while ROAD-R reports no significant reduction from t-norm losses — [Nandwani et al.](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2019/file/cf708fc1decf0337aded484f8f4519ae-Paper.pdf); [GLUECons](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.10914); contradicted by [ROAD-R](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.01597)

**Classical learning-plus-inference results**

- Punyakanok et al. (IJCAI 2005) define LO (learn without structure), L+I (inference only after learning) and IBT (inference in the training loop). Their claims: if local tasks are separable, L+I outperforms IBT; if the task is globally but not locally separable, IBT wins only with sufficient examples, and that number correlates with how inseparable the local classifiers are. With enough examples IBT beats L+I, but with a fixed number L+I can win — [Punyakanok et al.](https://scottyih.org/files/PRYZ05.pdf)
- Chang et al. (Machine Learning 2012), Table 9, structured perceptron F1 on citations at 5 / 10 / 20 / 300 labels: L 53.61 / 63.17 / 70.00 / 91.69; L+I 68.66 / 75.57 / 79.72 / 92.58; IBT 65.41 / 72.83 / 75.36 / 89.72; JIBT (learn violation penalties) 62.28 / 70.06 / 78.76 / 94.57. They state it is "often better to ignore the constraints when predicting the values ŷ during training, and to enforce the constraints only during inference" — [Chang et al.](https://cogcomp.seas.upenn.edu/papers/ChangRaRo12.pdf)
- Chang et al. also find learned soft constraints beat hard ones given enough labeled data, and that a single constraint can hurt (the citation `Start` constraint hurts semi-supervised performance) — [Chang et al.](https://cogcomp.seas.upenn.edu/papers/ChangRaRo12.pdf)
- Posterior regularization (Ganchev et al., JMLR 2010) separates the two effects in word alignment. Training with PR "significantly boosts" each model; projecting at decode time "always increases performance". The bijective constraint is more useful at training time (decode-only with regular training is worse than training with it), and the symmetric constraint is stronger at decode time. Where constraints cannot be evaluated at test time, they decode with p — [Ganchev et al.](https://jmlr.org/papers/volume11/ganchev10a/ganchev10a.pdf)
- Hu et al. is the neural form of the same idea: the teacher q is a posterior-regularization projection of the student p, the student is distilled from q in training, and either can be used at test. Accuracy on SST2 at 5 / 10 / 30 / 100% of labels: CNN 79.9 / 81.6 / 83.6 / 87.2; p 81.5 / 83.2 / 84.5 / 88.8; q 82.5 / 83.9 / 85.6 / 89.3. The q-p gap is larger on NER because q performs explicit joint inference — [Hu et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1603.06318)
- Structured Tuning (Li et al., ACL 2020) applies soft SRL constraints only in training and keeps Viterbi only for BIO validity. CoNLL-05 WSJ F1 goes from 87.85 to 88.03 with unique-role violations falling from 1.71 to 0.85. The authors "believe when we have the luxury of data, learning with constraints would become optional" — [Li et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.00496)
- Surveys state that loss-based methods cannot guarantee satisfaction — [Giunchiglia et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.00523)

### Inferences
- My tally of GLUECons Table 3 is less favourable than the authors' summary. Comparing each combination with ILP alone in the same column (strong baseline and low-data columns), SampL+ILP beats ILP alone in 5 of 14 cells and PD+ILP in 6 of 12. SampL is the closest relative of semantic loss in that table, so semantic loss on top of a hard layer is not a reliable accuracy win on that evidence. Its more dependable effect is lower pre-inference violation rates.
- The literature partly conflicts. Wang et al. find training does nearly everything and ILP adds little; GLUECons finds ILP alone usually wins; Primal-Dual and Chang find the answer moves with data size. A reading that reconciles them: when the base model is already close (strong model, clean local signal), inference is cheap and sufficient; when the base model is confused or data is thin, training has more to fix. This reconciliation is mine.
- Mapping to this repo, by analogy, since the classical results are for linear models and structured perceptrons: plain CE plus M1 is L+I; CE through the mask is IBT; CE plus M5 plus M1 is the CL+CI or CLCO pattern. The repo's low-data, small-model setting (d=32 weight files, a small hand-built corpus) is the regime where L+I and constrained learning have both tended to do well, which favours CE + M5 + M1 over training through the mask.
- For this project's prediction table: with M1 on, violations are 0 by construction for token-expressible constraints, so a 2x2 table on violation rate cannot show any effect of M5. The informative metrics are `massBefore` (M5 should lower it), violations with the mask off, M4-detected non-token-expressible violations, and held-out CE on targets that do not conflict with the KB.

### Gaps
- No paper found that studies the exact pipeline, including a soft bias plus a hard mask together with a post hoc verifier. No paper was found that gives a named method for it.
- I found no result that isolates a positive interaction (both better than the sum of the parts). Papers report totals only, and no significance test on the increment of "both" over "inference only" was found. The increments are often inside plausible noise (for example 78.63 against 78.72).
- Almost all direct comparisons are classifiers or taggers with a short output structure, not free-text generation.

## Do any autoregressive or transformer methods train with a logic loss and also decode with hard constraints, and what was measured?

### Takeaway
Yes, one direct precedent: Pseudo-Semantic Loss with Word Banning on GPT-2 (Ahmed, Chang, Van den Broeck, NeurIPS 2023). The combination gave the lowest toxicity, with most of the reduction coming from the training term, and the same paper shows the hard mask makes perplexity infinite on text containing banned words. The other decode-time methods named in the question (NeuroLogic, Outlines, FUDGE, GeDi, PPLM, DExperts) do not train the base model with a logic loss.

### Cited Findings
- Pseudo-semantic loss approximates the constraint's probability under an autoregressive model with a pseudolikelihood (a product of conditionals) centred on a model sample, L = -log E_{y~p} sum over y-tilde satisfying alpha of p-tilde_y(y-tilde). It penalises local perturbations of the sample that violate the constraint — [Ahmed et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.03905)
- Table 3 on RealToxicityPrompts (GPT-2, 124M parameters, Perspective API). Format: Expected Max Toxicity on Full / Toxic / Nontoxic prompts, then Toxicity Probability on the same, then perplexity — [Ahmed et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.03905)

| Model | EMT Full / Toxic / Nontoxic | Tox. prob. Full / Toxic / Nontoxic | PPL |
| ---- | ---- | ---- | ---- |
| GPT-2 | 0.44 / 0.62 / 0.39 | 34.11% / 67.27% / 24.85% | 25.85 |
| SGEAT | 0.32 / 0.46 / 0.28 | 14.05% / 35.72% / 7.99% | 28.72 |
| PseudoSL (with SGEAT) | 0.29 / 0.38 / 0.27 | 9.80% / 20.07% / 6.93% | 28.14 |
| GPT-2 + Word Banning | 0.40 / 0.55 / 0.36 | 27.92% / 57.86% / 19.56% | 22.24* |
| SGEAT + Word Banning | 0.30 / 0.41 / 0.27 | 10.73% / 27.05% / 6.17% | 24.91* |
| PseudoSL + Word Banning | 0.29 / 0.37 / 0.27 | 9.20% / 18.71% / 6.55% | 24.19* |

  *Measured only on the 50% least toxic portion of the validation set, so not comparable with the upper rows.
- The authors note that "even augmented with Word Banning, SGEAT exhibits higher toxicity than the base PseudoSL model", and that PseudoSL with Word Banning has the lowest overall toxicity, with the widest gap on toxic prompts. Banning here sets the probability of listed words to zero at decoding, using the same word list as the training constraint — [Ahmed et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.03905)
- The same paper tried NeuroLogic on the benchmark and estimated 165 hours on a 48GB GPU, with empty generations for at least 30% of a sampled prompt subset — [Ahmed et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.03905)
- PSL on small autoregressive tasks: Sudoku exact and consistent accuracy RNN 22.40% to 28.20% with PSL; Warcraft shortest path CNN-LSTM exact 62.00% to 66.00%, consistent 76.60% to 79.00%. Consistency stays well below 100%, as expected of a loss alone — [Ahmed et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.03905)
- Semantic Probabilistic Layers (not autoregressive) compile the constraint into the output layer, and training goes through it. Warcraft: ResNet-18 + semantic loss exact 59.4, consistent 61.2; + SPL exact 78.2, consistent 100.0. Simple Path: MLP + semantic loss exact 28.5, consistent 75.2; + SPL exact 37.6, consistent 100.0 — [Ahmed et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.00426)
- NeuroLogic Decoding converts hard logic constraints into a soft penalty in the decoding objective and uses beam search. CommonGen with supervised GPT2-L: beam search coverage 82.2%, Post and Vilar 97.4%, Hokamp and Liu 97.2%, NeuroLogic 97.7%. It is decode-only and coverage stays below 100% — [Lu et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.12884)
- Outlines formalises hard masking: a boolean mask restricts the support of the next-token categorical distribution, and an FSM over vocabulary strings decides each mask. It is decode-only — [Willard and Louf 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.09702)
- FUDGE reweights next-token probabilities with a future discriminator through a Bayesian factorisation. GeDi and DExperts steer with auxiliary models at decode time, and PPLM uses gradient-based steering. I checked these from abstracts and method summaries only, and none adds a logic loss to the base model's training — [FUDGE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.05218); [GeDi](https://arxiv.org/abs/2009.06367); [DExperts](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.03023); [PPLM](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.02164)
- DExperts Table on RealToxicityPrompts (avg. max toxicity / toxicity probability): GPT-2 0.527 / 0.520; PPLM 0.520 / 0.518; DAPT 0.428 / 0.360; GeDi 0.363 / 0.217; DExperts (large) 0.314 / 0.128. These use a different base model and setup from the PSL table, so the two tables cannot be compared directly — [DExperts](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.03023)
- LoCo-LMs (ICLR 2025) fine-tune LLMs with a semantic loss over truth beliefs from a KB of facts and rules, and report more self-consistency than an external solver (ConCoRD) on Macaw-Large. There is no decode-time mask — [Calanzone et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.13724)
- Dinu et al. (ACL 2019) are a non-logic analogue from translation, on a 2+2 layer transformer. Training the model to apply terminology gives 93 to 94% term use (train-by-replace) with BLEU +0.3 to -0.2, while constrained decoding reaches 99% on Wiktionary and 82% on IATE with BLEU -0.9 to -1.3 — [Dinu et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.01105)
- Hard decoding can hurt reasoning quality. CRANE reports that very restrictive grammars reduce problem-solving ability and gains up to 10 percentage points by relaxing the grammar during reasoning — [Banerjee et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.09061)
- A single-author preprint, not peer reviewed on the evidence I saw, reports that constrained decoding on 0.6B to 4B models lifts schema validity from 78.6 to 92.9% up to 100% while a semantic gap persists — [Chavan 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.23742)

### Inferences
- The PSL table suggests a useful pattern for this project: the training term moved Toxicity Probability on all prompts by far more (14.05% to 9.80%, or from GPT-2's 34.11%) than Word Banning did on top of it (9.80% to 9.20%). Do not over-read this. The metric is Perspective toxicity, not the constraint itself, so banning listed words cannot reach zero, and the SGEAT step confounds the PSL effect. Word-list violation counts are not reported.
- PSL's main difference from this repo's M5: PSL handles a constraint over the sequence and evaluates it around a model *sample*, whereas M5 is a per-position term on corpus prefixes. The sum over positions of -log P_A(t) is the probability of passing every local check along one fixed prefix. It is not the marginal probability that a generated sequence satisfies the KB.
- Corpus prefixes are a real limitation. A training mask precomputed per corpus position fires only where the corpus contains a trigger, while the inference mask fires on generated prefixes. PSL's sampling and SGEAT's self-generation are precedents for computing A_t on model-sampled prefixes as well.
- M2 (+lambda on supported tokens) is a constant-score relative of FUDGE and DExperts. It is equivalent to a log-prior odds shift, so it has no guarantee and it changes the distribution it is applied to.

### Gaps
- I found no paper that combines a logic loss with a soft logit bias and a hard mask, and none that reports a verifier check downstream of both.
- I could not establish from the PSL paper how Word Banning handles multi-token words. The paper does not specify this.
- Tiny transformers trained from scratch are not represented. All results use pretrained GPT-2 or larger, or non-text tasks.

## Should the mask be on or off while computing the training loss, and what happens when a target is outside the allowed set?

### Takeaway
Compute CE and semantic loss on the unmasked logits, and apply the mask only at inference. If the mask is on, the semantic-loss term is exactly 0 with zero gradient. The papers that do train through a hard layer (SPL, CCN, RL masking) are a coherent alternative, but they drop semantic loss and, in the CCN case, change the loss. Plain CE already contains the semantic loss with weight 1 at positions where the target is allowed (my derivation), so lambda_s is an extra weight on a term CE already has. When a target is outside A_t, masked CE is infinite, so that conflict has to be handled explicitly.

### Cited Findings
- Semantic loss is zero for an assignment that satisfies the constraint (Proposition 3, Satisfaction) — [Xu et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.11157)
- Huang and Ontañón (2020) show that masking is a state-dependent differentiable function applied in computing the policy, so the gradient through the mask is a valid policy gradient for the masked policy. Masked logits get zero gradient. They use a large finite negative M (for example -1e8), which leaves a "virtually zero" probability — [Huang and Ontañón](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.14171)
- "Naive" masking in the same paper samples from the masked distribution but takes the gradient from the unmasked one. This is the closest RL analogue of "train unmasked, deploy masked" (my analogy), though it is not the same as CE because RL's samples and rewards come from the masked policy. It had the best episodic return on almost all maps, but average KL divergence between policy updates was significantly higher, and time to solve was volatile (49.14% of training on the 24x24 map, against about 12% for proper masking) — [Huang and Ontañón](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.14171)
- Penalty alone (a soft training term only, by my analogy) "does not scale": with r = -0.01 on a 10x10 map, agents took 3.43% of training to find the first reward, against about 0.06% with masking, and the penalty value was hard to tune. An agent trained with masking still behaves usefully with the mask removed, but executes more invalid actions as the map grows — [Huang and Ontañón](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.14171)
- Training through a hard layer naively can give wrong gradients. In C-HMCNN with hA=0.3, hB=0.1, yA=0, yB=1, standard BCE through the constraint module gives a gradient that increases hA and leaves hB unchanged, because the max takes the wrong path. The authors introduce MCLoss to fix this, with gradients of about +1.4 and -10 — [Giunchiglia and Lukasiewicz 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.10151)
- CCN(h), the extension of this to hard logical constraints, is reported to beat multilabel models with post-processing on 16 benchmarks. The authors state the constraint layer is not a simple post-processing step — [Giunchiglia and Lukasiewicz 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.13427)
- SPL trains through the constrained layer (see the numbers above), and that layer cannot allocate probability mass to inconsistent predictions — [Ahmed et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.00426)
- A hard mask makes likelihood on conflicting data infinite. PSL: "Setting the probabilities of toxic words to zero" sends perplexity to infinity, so they evaluate it on only the least toxic half — [Ahmed et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.03905)
- ROAD-R avoids conflict by construction: it keeps only requirements that are always satisfied by the ground-truth labels of the dataset — [ROAD-R](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.01597)
- Gold data can violate plausible constraints. Structured Tuning reports oracle error rates of 0.40% (unique roles) and 2.34% (frame roles) on the CoNLL-05 development set, while the larger CoNLL-12 oracle has no such violations on development data — [Li et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.00496)
- Soft treatments for conflicts: Hu et al. project with slack variables, min KL(q||p) + C * sum of slacks, with rule strength controlling it — [Hu et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1603.06318). Chang et al. learn the violation penalty rho from data (JIBT), and rho = infinity gives hard constraints — [Chang et al.](https://cogcomp.seas.upenn.edu/papers/ChangRaRo12.pdf)
- The repo's M5 design already computes the term from unmasked `probs` and already counts corpus conflicts (for example `the girl -> flew`), noting that CE and L_sem pull in opposite directions at those positions — [`docs/progress/07-neurosymbolic-code-2b.md`](../../docs/progress/07-neurosymbolic-code-2b.md)

### Inferences
All algebra here is mine. I checked the identity and gradients numerically with finite differences (logits [2, 1, 0.5, -1, 0.2], A = {0, 2, 4}, target 2): CE 2.091167 equals masked CE + SL 2.091167; the gradient of CE equals the gradient of masked CE plus the gradient of SL in every component; and SL on the masked distribution is 0.

- **Degeneracy.** If training applies M1 and then computes L_sem, then P_A = 1, L_sem = 0 and its gradient is 0. So "same logits for both, mask on" silently removes M5. A unit test can catch this: with the mask on, `semLoss` must be exactly 0, and with the mask off it must match the known 0.4055 case.
- **Recommended ordering for this repo.** Training: logits, then CE + lambda_s * L_sem on the unmasked distribution, with no M1 or M2. Inference: logits, then M1, M2, temperature, top-p, sample, then M4. Evaluate as a 2x2 of {M5 off, on} by {M1 off, on} at the same seeds, on the metrics listed in the first section. This is the CL+CI or CLCO layout from the first section, applied to tokens.
- **Decomposition.** For a target y in A_t, with p unmasked and p^m masked:

  CE = -log p_y = -log(p_y / P_A) - log P_A = (masked CE) + (semantic loss)

  So CE + lambda * SL = masked CE + (1 + lambda) * SL. All three training choices are one family, masked CE + w * SL: w=0 is training through the mask (IBT-like), w=1 is plain CE (L+I), and w>1 is CE plus M5 (CL). This gives one knob for comparing them. I did not find it stated in the papers I read.
- **What each gets from the gradient.** Masked CE gives banned logits exactly zero gradient (numeric check: 0.0), so nothing pushes mass off banned tokens. This matches Huang's result. A model trained that way depends on the mask, and `massBefore` is free to rise. That is acceptable if the mask is permanent and you only care about the distribution over A. It conflicts with this project's interest in showing the KB reaching the weights.
- **Consequence for M5's value.** At positions with an allowed target, M5 only re-weights something CE already optimises. Its distinct contributions must come from contexts that lack a target or do not have one that settles the question, such as model-sampled prefixes or KB-derived prompts. This matches Xu et al.'s largest gains being in semi-supervised settings and matches GLUECons's arithmetic result. Applying M5 only on corpus positions may produce a small effect, which is itself a testable prediction.
- **Conflict position.** When y is not in A_t (and the context always has that target), minimising -log p_y - lambda * log(1 - p_y) gives p_y = 1 / (1 + lambda). So at a true conflict, lambda_s = 1 leaves 50% on the banned target and lambda_s = 4 leaves 20%. This gives lambda_s a concrete reading. On a fixed lambda: Primal-Dual learns the multiplier by dual ascent, which is an alternative if tuning proves hard.
- **Options for conflicts, in my order of preference for this repo.** (1) Report and inspect each conflict, since each is either a KB error or a corpus exception, and keep the count as a metric. (2) Remove the rule if the corpus is right (ROAD-R's approach), or keep a soft M5 term (Hu, Chang). (3) Do not use masked CE at those positions, because it is infinite. (4) When reporting perplexity with the mask on, follow PSL and exclude or separately report conflicting targets, and say so.
- **M2 in training.** Menon et al. treat an additive logit offset as something applied *either* post hoc or in the loss ([Menon et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.07314)). By analogy, if +lambda were included during training, the model would learn around it, and adding it again at inference without having trained with it is a prior shift. Keep M2 out of training unless it is deliberately studied, and do not stack the two without comparing them.

### Gaps
- "Inference-masked loss" is a term from the brief. I could not verify it as a named method. Structured Tuning does not use it. It applies soft constraints in the loss and only Viterbi at decoding.
- I found no paper that analyses semantic-loss gradients under an inference mask for a language model. The degeneracy is simple algebra, and I found no paper stating it for this case.
- The Huang and Ontañón results are RL on a game, so they transfer to supervised next-token training only by analogy.

## Is it standard to use one constraint compiler for both loss and mask, and how sound is M1 when the KB is incomplete or constraints are not token-level?

### Takeaway
Sharing one constraint *source* is common, and SPL and CCN share a compiled object by construction, but no paper I found presents one compiler as a standard or best practice. Some strong results deliberately use different constraint sets per layer. M1's guarantee is relative to the KB and tokenization: it is a guarantee about the tokens the KB bans, not about truth.

### Cited Findings
- Same knowledge, same object: SPL's constraint circuit cK multiplies the predictive distribution, cutting the support, both in training and at test. Consistency is guaranteed by that component — [Ahmed et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.00426)
- Same requirement set applied in both layers: ROAD-R uses the same set Pi for t-norm loss and MaxSAT correction — [ROAD-R](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.01597). PSL uses the same word list for its loss and for Word Banning — [Ahmed et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.03905). In Hu et al. q is built from the same rules that train p — [Hu et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1603.06318)
- Same knowledge, different compilation: GLUECons's Table 2 shows PD and ILP need conversion for logical constraints while SampL handles them directly, and each method has its own expressive limits — [GLUECons](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.10914). ROAD-R uses fuzzy t-norm relaxations for the loss and MaxSAT for the output — [ROAD-R](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.01597)
- Different constraints per layer, deliberately: Structured Tuning uses soft semantic constraints in the loss and Viterbi only for BIO validity — [Li et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.00496). Primal-Dual's SRL inference enforces transitions only, while training includes other constraints, leaving thousands of violations after inference — [Nandwani et al.](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2019/file/cf708fc1decf0337aded484f8f4519ae-Paper.pdf)
- Ganchev et al. show that different constraints are stronger in different places (bijective in training, symmetric at decode), and some constraints simply cannot be evaluated at test time — [Ganchev et al.](https://jmlr.org/papers/volume11/ganchev10a/ganchev10a.pdf)
- Exact satisfaction of a sequence-level constraint under an autoregressive distribution is hard, which is the motivation for PSL's approximation — [Ahmed et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.03905)
- Finding a minimal correction of a non-admissible prediction is NP-complete in general (ROAD-R Theorems 5.1 and 5.2), so post hoc repair is a separate problem from masking — [ROAD-R](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.01597)
- Greedy masking treats every still-viable prefix as having expected future grammaticality 1, which GAD identifies as a source of substantial sampling bias: a prefix that looks allowed may have little probable valid continuation — [Park et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.21047)
- The repo's plan uses one function, `allowed()`, to produce banned and supported tokens, consumed by the training masks and by the inference processor, and notes that banned sets are stored because they are small and sparse — [`docs/progress/07-neurosymbolic-code-2b.md`](../../docs/progress/07-neurosymbolic-code-2b.md)

### Inferences
- A single `allowed()` is the right default for this repo. It keeps the comparison clean and avoids a whole class of bugs where training and inference disagree. Evaluate it on the same prefix type you intend to measure, though. The function can be shared while its inputs (corpus prefixes in training, generated prefixes at inference) still differ.
- One correction to the phrase "sound when the KB is complete". There are two distinct properties:
  - With a **denylist** (the repo stores banned tokens; A_t is everything else), M1 is sound with respect to what the KB derives. It never emits a token the KB bans at that step. If the KB does not contain a fact, the corresponding wrong token passes. This is an open-world mask: sound relative to the KB, incomplete relative to truth.
  - With an **allowlist** (closed-world), no unsupported token passes, but true statements the KB does not know are blocked.

  So a KB gap appears as a false pass under a denylist and as an over-block under an allowlist. M4 is the check on the first, and `corpusConflicts` is an early warning on the second.
- A shared A_t also gives M5 a limit: under a denylist, semantic loss only teaches "avoid these tokens". It does not teach which allowed token is right ("no reason to prefer one allowed token over another", as the repo's chapter says). That is why M5 cannot replace CE and cannot turn an incomplete KB into a complete one.
- M1's guarantee also depends on trigger matching. A rule that does not fire because the prefix phrasing differs produces no ban at all, and neither M1 nor M5 acts on it. Only M4 can catch that after the fact. Violations like this should be counted as "non-token-expressible" in P1 rather than hidden.
- Suggested reporting for a shared compiler: for every `allowed()` call log fired rules, banned set size, whether the set was unenforceable (the plan already has an `unenforceable` field), and whether the target conflicted. This gives M3 the same vocabulary across training and inference.

### Gaps
- I found no survey or paper that explicitly recommends one compiler for both, and none that measures the cost of letting them drift apart.
- I found no paper that measures how M1-style denylist soundness degrades with KB completeness. The denylist and allowlist analysis above is my own reasoning.

## Does temperature and top-p preserve the hard-mask guarantee, and what about multi-token words?

### Takeaway
Yes for the support guarantee, provided the mask is applied to logits before temperature, softmax and top-p. The guarantee holds per token and only for what the mask covers. It does not preserve the model's conditional distribution, and it does not reach constraints that span BPE tokens unless the mask is compiled against token strings. I found no paper testing temperature or top-p interaction specifically, so the support result is simple arithmetic plus a check of reference code. The all-banned case is a real failure path in this repo's sampler.

### Cited Findings
- Hugging Face's `TopPLogitsWarper` computes softmax over the current scores, sorts them, removes tokens whose cumulative mass is within 1 - top_p, and keeps at least `min_tokens_to_keep`. Its processors use `-float("inf")` for suppression — [transformers `logits_process.py`](https://github.com/huggingface/transformers/blob/main/src/transformers/generation/logits_process.py)
- `InfNanRemoveLogitsProcessor` exists to avoid generation failing on inf or NaN, and its documentation says there should be no correct flag combination that warrants it, which indicates NaN handling is treated as exceptional — [transformers `logits_process.py`](https://github.com/huggingface/transformers/blob/main/src/transformers/generation/logits_process.py)
- A finite-M mask gives a small non-zero probability rather than zero — [Huang and Ontañón](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.14171)
- Constrained decoding "can distort the LLM's distribution", so outputs are valid but not proportional to the model's conditional probability. On some benchmarks ASAp's KL to the target converges to 0 while GCD's does not; on others it barely improves after 2,000 iterations. It produces correct samples 38% more often than GCD (geomean) on INV-BV4, but 73% less often on the SLIA benchmarks both can solve, so aligning the distribution did not uniformly improve correctness — [Park et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.21047)
- Token misalignment: because subword tokens do not align with grammar terminals, naive masking can force a different tokenization even when the unconstrained output was valid. On JSON-encoded GSM8K with Mistral 7B (five-shot), accuracy was 41.5% unconstrained, 30.8% naive constrained, and 41.8% with minimally invasive constraining — [Beurer-Kellner et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.06988)
- Outlines compiles the constraint to an FSM and indexes each vocabulary *string* against it, so a multi-character token is allowed if and only if its characters keep the FSM valid. This is how it handles tokens that do not align with the constraint's units — [Willard and Louf 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.09702)
- Hugging Face's `NoBadWordsLogitsProcessor` bans token-id sequences, and it warns that users must obtain ids with the correct prefix-space behaviour because of how tokenizers treat a leading space. `PrefixConstrainedLogitsProcessor` is the allowlist form, taking a function from generated ids to allowed tokens — [transformers `logits_process.py`](https://github.com/huggingface/transformers/blob/main/src/transformers/generation/logits_process.py)
- The repo already records the limit: "A banned word that BPE splits into pieces is constrained only on its first piece" — [`docs/progress/README.md`](../../docs/progress/README.md)
- The repo's generate loop divides logits by temperature, subtracts the row max before `exp`, normalises, sorts, takes a nucleus until cumulative probability reaches `topP`, and defaults to `nucleus[0]` — [`src/routes/train-transformer/transformer.ts`](../../src/routes/train-transformer/transformer.ts)

### Inferences
- **Why order mask, then temperature, then top-p keeps the support.** -inf / T = -inf for any T > 0; softmax gives exactly 0; top-p cannot add mass to a zero-probability token. Numeric check with T = 0.5, 1 and 2: banned entries stay exactly 0.0, and allowed entries remain positive and sum to 1. Masking the logits before softmax is the same as renormalising over A_t, so no separate renormalisation is needed.
- **Why masking after top-p is the wrong order.** The nucleus would be selected using mass on banned tokens. If a banned token holds 0.95 of the mass and topP = 0.9, the nucleus is that token alone, and masking leaves nothing to sample. In other cases it silently shrinks the nucleus. The repo's order (mask first, as in the README's flow) avoids this.
- **M2 and temperature.** Because the bias comes before temperature, its effect on odds is exp(lambda / T), not exp(lambda). A lambda tuned at T=1 is stronger at T=0.8. M1 and M2 commute on finite entries (-inf + lambda = -inf). Record `massBefore` on unmasked logits at T=1 so it is comparable across temperature settings and comparable with M5's P_A.
- **All-banned failure in this repo.** I tested the arithmetic in Node: if every logit is -Infinity, `max` stays -Infinity, `logits[v] - max` is NaN, and `probs` are all NaN. From reading the code, the nucleus loop then never reaches `topP`, `r < cumulative` is always false, and `sampled` stays `nucleus[0].idx`, a banned token, with no error. That would silently refute P1. Add a guard: if A_t is empty, stop or fall back and record it in the M3 trace. This code path does not exist until M1 is added, and I did not run the sampler itself with a mask. A test that bans every token is the cheap check.
- **Finite masks.** Use real -Infinity rather than a large finite number, since this makes the guarantee exact, and it keeps P1 a true zero under high temperature.
- **Multi-token words: a mask rule.** Banning only the first BPE piece both under-blocks and over-blocks:
  - It under-blocks because the same word can be reached by other segmentations or casing, and token-id bans are not equivalent to string bans.
  - It over-blocks because every other word sharing that first piece is blocked too, which is the same invasiveness DOMINO measures.

  The string-level fix is the Outlines idea on a small scale: compile the banned words into a trie or automaton over characters, then at each step test each vocabulary token's string against the current partial word. Block a token only if it completes a banned word, or if it can only lead to one. This requires tracking partial-word state from the generated prefix. Apply the same check at word boundaries, because the tokenizer's handling of word ends decides what counts as a finished word.
- **Multi-token words in the loss.** A_t in M5 must come from the same automaton, so at mid-word positions it reflects continuations that are still legal. Otherwise M5 and M1 disagree on exactly the positions that matter. Note the semantic gap: for a word split in pieces, P_A at the first piece is a one-step quantity, which is an approximation of the probability the whole word is allowed. That is GAD's distortion problem on a small scale, and it is a reason to keep M4 as the final check.
- **Practical scope for this repo.** A hand-written KB with few relations and a small vocabulary is the easy case. Whole-word tokens are a real option if BPE merges are chosen or checked for KB words. Count KB words that BPE splits and report them in `unenforceable`. A trie is justified only if that count is non-trivial.
- **What "P1 = 0 violations" can honestly claim.** Zero banned tokens emitted under a covered trigger. It does not claim zero false statements, which is what M4 measures, and M4's parser has its own errors.

### Gaps
- I found no paper that specifically measures how temperature or top-p changes constrained decoding quality. The closest is a temperature robustness probe in the small-LLM preprint, on structural failure only ([Chavan 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.23742)).
- I found no paper that measures word-level KB constraints under BPE on a tiny from-scratch model. The grammar-decoding work is on JSON and code with large pretrained models.
- I did not verify how RealToxicityPrompts-style Word Banning treats multi-token words at the implementation level.
