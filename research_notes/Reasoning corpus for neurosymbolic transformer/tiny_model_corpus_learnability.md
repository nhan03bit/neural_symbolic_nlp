# Learnability of a tiny synthetic reasoning corpus for a from-scratch micro-transformer

Target setting, for reference throughout: a decoder-only model with d=32, 2 heads, FF 128, 2–6 layers, context 32, and about 30k–100k parameters. It trains with Adam at lr 1e-3, one full-batch step per epoch, for 300–2000 epochs. The corpus is about 3–5k tokens with a BPE vocabulary of about 100–400 tokens.

Scale caveat: every paper below uses models 4x to 10,000x larger than this one, and almost all use corpora far bigger than 5k tokens. Where an inference transfers a result down to this scale, it is marked as an extrapolation.

## Q1. TinyStories: what vocabulary and size let 1M–30M parameter models be coherent, and what does it say about reasoning-like consistency at small scale?

### Takeaway
TinyStories reaches coherence by shrinking the *language*: about 1,500 basic words and a 10k-token tokenizer. It does not shrink the model below about 1M parameters. Grammar is learned first and by the smallest models. Consistency and simple reasoning appear only at larger hidden sizes (64→128) and with more depth. A d=32 model is below the width at which TinyStories saw consistency emerge.

### Cited Findings
- The stories were generated by GPT-3.5/GPT-4 from a word list of about 1,500 basic words a 3–4-year-old would know. Each generation draws one random verb, one noun and one adjective that must appear in the story, which forces diversity. — [Eldan & Li 2023, TinyStories (ar5iv)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2305.07759)
- The tokenizer is GPT-Neo's, restricted to the top 10K tokens. The architecture is GPT-Neo with context 512. Models range from about 1M to 35M parameters, with named sizes 1M, 2.5M, 8.3M, 21M (1 layer), 28M and 33M. — [TinyStories (ar5iv)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2305.07759)
- Models under 10M parameters, some with a single transformer block, produce fluent multi-paragraph stories with almost perfect grammar. By contrast, about 125M-parameter GPT-Neo/GPT-2 models trained on general text rarely produce coherent text. — [TinyStories abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.07759)
- Grammar is learned by relatively small models and plateaus earliest. Consistency with the story beginning appears when hidden size grows from 64 to 128. — [TinyStories (ar5iv)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2305.07759)
- Depth versus width:
  - Shallower models do relatively better on grammar than on consistency.
  - One-layer models struggle with instruction-following, and two layers give some ability.
  - Factual knowledge relies more on embedding width, while context-tracking relies more on layers.

  — [TinyStories (ar5iv)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2305.07759)
- Very small models (2.5M) and one-layer models can attain factual knowledge, and slightly larger models show some reasoning. The 1M, 8-layer model failed every factual prompt. — [TinyStories (ar5iv)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2305.07759)
- The canonical reasoning probe is disjunction elimination: "Lily wanted to get either a cat or a dog. Her mother didn't let her get a dog so instead she …" → cat. — [TinyStories (ar5iv)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2305.07759)
- Evaluation used about 50 hand-written story beginnings, 10 completions each at temperature 1, graded by GPT-4 on grammar, creativity and consistency. ROUGE overlap was used to argue the outputs were not memorised. — [TinyStories (ar5iv)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2305.07759)

### Inferences
- The transferable lesson is to restrict the language, not just the model. The target model is about 10–300x smaller than the smallest TinyStories model, so the corpus vocabulary should be far smaller than 1,500 words. *Extrapolation:* tens of content words, a handful of relation and function words, and fixed sentence frames.
- d=32 is half of the d=64 at which TinyStories consistency was still weak. Expect grammar and template learning to work, but expect cross-sentence consistency (carrying an entity's property forward) to be the bottleneck. Deeper variants (4–6 layers) should help context-tracking more than adding width would. This is an extrapolation from TinyStories' depth-versus-width results.
- TinyStories' "diversity by forced random word inclusion" is a useful pattern. Generate sentences by sampling (entity, category, attribute) combinations from a knowledge base, not by hand-writing a few examples.

### Gaps
- The fetched text did not give the total TinyStories dataset size in stories or tokens, or training step counts. It is certainly orders of magnitude larger than 5k tokens, but I did not verify the exact figure.
- No result in TinyStories covers models under 1M parameters or corpora under 10k tokens.

## Q2. Small transformers on synthetic tasks (arithmetic, grokking, induction heads): how many steps or examples are needed, and does format matter?

### Takeaway
Format matters a great deal. Intermediate steps written out in the order a left-to-right model computes them can cut the examples needed by an order of magnitude. Generalisation from small data (as opposed to memorisation) typically needs far more optimisation steps than memorisation, often 10^4–10^6, plus weight decay. In-context copying ("induction") requires at least 2 layers.

### Cited Findings
**Lee et al. 2023, "Teaching Arithmetic to Small Transformers"**
- Setup: NanoGPT with 6 layers, 6 heads, d=384, about 10.6M parameters. Tokenisation is character-level with absolute positions. — [Lee et al. (ar5iv)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2307.03381)
- 3-digit addition, by format:
  - **Plain** (`128+367=495`) plateaus at a little over 85% even with 10,000 examples.
  - **Reverse** output (`$128+367=594$`) shows a sharp phase transition between 1,000 and 4,000 examples, reaching near-perfect accuracy around 2,500.
  - **Simplified scratchpad** (per-digit sum and carry) reaches 100% with 2,000 examples.
  - **Detailed scratchpad** reaches 100% with 1,000 examples.

  — [Lee et al. (ar5iv)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2307.03381)
- Internal inconsistency: the paper's text elsewhere says reverse needs more than double the 2,000 examples used by simplified scratchpad, which conflicts with the 2,500 figure in the figure caption. — [Lee et al. (ar5iv)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2307.03381)
- Balanced sampling helped. Their 10k set contained all 100 one-digit sums, 900 two-digit sums and 9,000 three-digit sums, with about 2,500 examples each for 0, 1, 2 and 3 carries. This beat uniform random sampling. — [Lee et al. (ar5iv)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2307.03381)
- The phase transition is linked to low-rank matrix completion. For 2-digit addition, both NanoGPT and matrix completion start learning near 1,500 examples. — [Lee et al. (ar5iv)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2307.03381)
- Models do not length-generalise. They learn "a mapping function constrained to trained digit lengths". — [Lee et al. (ar5iv)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2307.03381)
- Chain-of-thought-style data improves accuracy, sample complexity and convergence speed. — [Lee et al. abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.03381)

**Grokking: Power et al. 2022; Nanda et al. 2023**
- Power et al. setup: a 2-layer transformer, width 128, 4 heads, about 4×10^5 non-embedding parameters. Optimiser: AdamW, lr 1e-3, weight decay 1, β2 = 0.98. The default budget was 10^5 updates. — [Power et al. (ar5iv)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2201.02177)
- Modular division at 50% training data: training accuracy is near-perfect by about 10^3 steps, but validation takes about 10^6 steps to catch up, with little generalisation before about 10^5. — [Power et al. (ar5iv)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2201.02177)
- For S5 composition near 25–30% data, removing 1% of the data raised the median steps to generalise by about 40–50%. — [Power et al. (ar5iv)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2201.02177)
- Some operations, such as x³+xy²+y mod 97, never generalised at any data fraction up to 95%. — [Power et al. (ar5iv)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2201.02177)
- Weight decay was the most effective intervention: it more than halved the data needed. The learning rate had to fall within about one order of magnitude for generalisation to occur. — [Power et al. (ar5iv)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2201.02177)
- Nanda et al. mainline setup:
  - A 1-layer transformer with d=128, 4 heads and 512 MLP units.
  - The task is (a+b) mod 113, trained on 30% of the pairs.
  - Training is **full-batch AdamW at lr 1e-3, weight decay 1.0, for 40,000 epochs**.

  — [Nanda et al. (ar5iv)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2301.05217)
- Nanda et al.'s grokking timeline:
  - Memorisation occurs over epochs 0–1.4k.
  - The generalising circuit forms over 1.4k–9.4k, while train and test loss stay flat.
  - "Cleanup" and the test-accuracy jump happen over 9.4k–14k.

  — [Nanda et al. (ar5iv)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2301.05217)
- The networks did not grok without regularisation such as weight decay, and smaller weight decay made grokking much slower. With enough training data the train–test gap disappears. — [Nanda et al. (ar5iv)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2301.05217)

**Induction heads: Olsson et al. 2022**
- Induction heads implement `[A][B] … [A] → [B]`. They need two heads in different layers, so they do not appear in 1-layer models. — [Olsson et al., transformer-circuits.pub](https://transformer-circuits.pub/2022/in-context-learning-and-induction-heads/index.html)
- Every multi-layer model tested shows a "phase change" early in training. It is visible as a bump in the training loss, and it is when most in-context learning ability and the induction heads form. — [Olsson et al.](https://transformer-circuits.pub/2022/in-context-learning-and-induction-heads/index.html)
- Their ICL score is the loss at token 500 minus the loss at token 50, averaged over examples. — [Olsson et al.](https://transformer-circuits.pub/2022/in-context-learning-and-induction-heads/index.html)

### Inferences
- **Optimisation budget mismatch (extrapolation).** The target schedule is 300–2000 full-batch Adam steps with no weight decay. Nanda's comparable full-batch, lr-1e-3 setup needed about 10k epochs *with* weight decay 1.0 to generalise, and Power et al. needed 10^5–10^6 steps. Within 2000 epochs, expect memorisation of the training sentences, not a grokked rule.
- If held-out generalisation is the goal, switch to AdamW with weight decay in the 0.1–1.0 range and allow at least 10^4 epochs. Alternatively, make the rule shallow enough to be learned during the memorisation phase; see the in-context design in Q4.
- **Format rule (from Lee et al.).** Write every intermediate step explicitly, in the order a causal model would compute it. "x is a c . c can a . so x can a ." is the analogue of the detailed scratchpad, and "x can a" alone is the analogue of plain format. Lee et al. found the scratchpad version needs about 10x fewer examples, although each example is longer.
- **Layers (from Olsson et al.).** Any design where the model must copy an entity token from earlier in the window needs at least 2 layers, so the 1-layer extreme of the range should not be used. The 2-layer minimum in the target range is adequate in principle.
- **Balanced sampling (from Lee et al.).** Balance the corpus over reasoning types and over categories and attributes, the analogue of balancing carries. Do not let one category dominate.
- **Expect sharp transitions.** Evaluate at several checkpoints (for example every 100 epochs), not only at the end.

### Gaps
- I found no published result for induction-head or rule formation in models at about 50k parameters with a 32-token context on a corpus of about 4k tokens. Step counts at this scale must be measured empirically.
- I did not verify the training-token count at which Olsson's phase change occurs.

## Q3. Physics of Language Models (Allen-Zhu & Li) and the reversal curse: why must facts appear in varied forms, and what does this imply for held-out facts?

### Takeaway
A fact that appears in only one fixed sentence form tends to be memorised as a string, not stored as a queryable attribute of the entity. Extraction accuracy for such facts on held-out people can be about 10% or lower. Stating each fact in several paraphrases and orders raises it to about 87–97%. Knowledge is also directional: a model trained only on "A r B" essentially cannot answer "B r⁻¹ ?". So every query direction you plan to evaluate must appear in training for *some* entities.

### Cited Findings
- Setup (bioS):
  - 100,000 synthetic people, each with 6 attributes (birth date, city, university, major, employer, company city).
  - The model is GPT-2-style with 12 layers, d=768 and 124M parameters.
  - Pretraining took 80k steps at batch 96, about 540 passes over the data.

  — [Allen-Zhu & Li, Physics of LMs 3.1 (ar5iv)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2309.14316)
- Held-out protocol: all biographies are in pretraining. QA pairs for half the people (P_train) are used for fine-tuning, and accuracy is measured on QA for the other half (P_test), whose QA forms were never seen. — [Physics 3.1 (ar5iv)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2309.14316)
- QA accuracy on held-out people:
  - Pretrain on one biography per person ("bioS single"), then QA fine-tune: **9.7%**.
  - Five varied-template biographies per person ("multi5"): about 41%.
  - multi5 plus sentence permutation: **96.6%**.
  - "single+permute5": 4.4% → 70%.

  — [Physics 3.1 (ar5iv)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2309.14316)
- "Celebrity" effect: when only a subset of people is augmented, held-out accuracy for the *non-augmented* minority still rises from 4.4% to 86.8% on bioS, and from 10.0% to 76.3% on bioR. — [Physics 3.1 (ar5iv)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2309.14316)
- Mixed training: putting biographies plus QA for half the people into *pretraining* gives 86.6% on held-out people for bioS single. — [Physics 3.1 (ar5iv)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2309.14316)
- Without augmentation a model may memorise knowledge but extract none of it, with accuracy reaching 0%, and later instruction fine-tuning does not fix this. Knowledge is near-linearly encoded in the hidden states of the entity-name tokens when augmented. — [Physics 3.1 abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.14316)
- Physics 3.2 (knowledge manipulation):
  - Without chain-of-thought, even a single-step derived judgement is hard: "is the birth month even?" needed about 10,000 training samples to reach 75%.
  - Inverse search was near 0% across training methods, augmentations and model sizes. It worked only when the fact also appeared in reverse order in pretraining.
  - CoT used at training time did not help no-CoT test accuracy.

  — [Physics 3.2 (ar5iv)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2309.14402)
- Reversal curse: models fine-tuned on "A is B" fictitious facts (GPT-3, Llama-1) fail on "B is ?", and data augmentation does not alleviate this. GPT-4 answers real celebrity-parent questions 79% of the time forward versus 33% in reverse. If "A is B" is *in the context*, models can deduce the reverse. — [Berglund et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.12288)

### Inferences
- **Knowledge augmentation for a tiny corpus (extrapolation from 124M parameters to about 50k).** State each atomic KB fact in 2–5 surface forms, for example:
  - "rex is a dog ."
  - "the dog rex …"
  - "rex , a dog , …"

  Also vary sentence order across "paragraphs". Physics 3.1 suggests this is what turns memorised strings into extractable attributes. On a 4k-token budget this limits the KB to roughly 50–150 atomic facts.
- **Use the celebrity effect to save tokens.** Augment only a subset of entities heavily, for example half of them in 3–5 forms. Give the rest a single form. Physics 3.1 found that the non-augmented entities still become extractable.
- **Put the evaluation format into pretraining for some entities.** This is the "mixed training" result: if the probe is "rex can", the corpus must contain "X can a" sentences for *other* entities.
- **Direction is fixed.** Never evaluate "what is a dog? → rex" unless some entities appear in that order in training. This applies to both Physics 3.2 inverse search and the reversal curse.
- **Prefer premises in the window to parametric recall.** In-context premises escape the reversal curse (Berglund et al.), and they fit a 32-token window if each step is short.
- **Entity tokenisation.** Physics 3.1 finds knowledge attaches to the entity-name token representations. Make entity names single BPE tokens if possible, which is feasible with a vocabulary of 100–400. Multi-token names add a split-attention burden for a d=32 model. This is an inference, not tested at this scale.

### Gaps
- No Physics-of-LMs experiment used models under about 100M parameters, and the papers do not report a required number of exposures per fact.
- Accuracy numbers for the reversal-curse fictitious-fact experiments and the exact model sizes were not available in the abstract I fetched.

## Q4. Rule-following and compositional generalisation in tiny models: can they learn "x is a c, c can a, so x can a" for unseen x? What do SCAN and COGS say?

### Takeaway
Small seq2seq models generalise almost perfectly to *new combinations of seen parts* when the training data covers the space densely. They fail badly (0–35%) when a primitive seen only in isolation must enter a composed rule. Transformers trained to grok two-hop composition generalise in-distribution but fail out-of-distribution, when the held-out entity's facts were never used in a composed form. In contrast, comparison-type rules generalise OOD.

### Cited Findings
- SCAN contains 20,910 commands, with a 13-word input vocabulary and 6 output actions. — [Lake & Baroni 2018 (ar5iv)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1711.00350)
- On a random 80/20 split the best model reaches 99.8%. — [Lake & Baroni (ar5iv)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1711.00350)
- Accuracy by training fraction: 1% (about 210 examples) gives about 5%, 2% gives about 54%, and 4% gives about 93%. — [Lake & Baroni (ar5iv)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1711.00350)
- The overall-best model is a 2-layer LSTM with 200 units. — [Lake & Baroni (ar5iv)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1711.00350)
- Length split: 13.8–20.8%. — [Lake & Baroni (ar5iv)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1711.00350)
- Add-primitive "jump" (seen only in isolation and tested in compositions): 0.08–1.2%, whereas "turn left" reaches about 90%. — [Lake & Baroni (ar5iv)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1711.00350)
- Adding composed "jump" examples to training raises "jump" accuracy to 38.3% with 8 examples, 77.8% with 16, and 88.4% with 32. — [Lake & Baroni (ar5iv)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1711.00350)
- COGS: Transformers, BiLSTMs and LSTMs reach 96–99% in distribution but only 16–35% on the generalisation set, with ±6–8% variation across seeds. Structural generalisation is harder than lexical generalisation. Training covers recursion depth 0–2, while the test set covers 3–12. Primitives appear in training "as isolated words, but not as a part of a sentence". — [Kim & Linzen 2020 (ar5iv)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2010.05465)
- Grokked implicit reasoning (Wang et al. 2024): two-hop composition data construction:
  - A random knowledge graph with 2,000 entities and 200 relations, with 20 relations per entity.
  - Atomic facts are split 95% ID and 5% OOD.
  - Training includes *all* atomic facts and a fraction of the ID-inferred facts.
  - The OOD test uses inferred facts whose atomic facts were only ever seen in atomic form.

  — [Wang et al. 2024 (arXiv HTML)](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.15071)
- Wang et al. results:
  - Composition: ID generalisation emerges via grokking, with training saturating around 14k steps and generalisation improving for about 50x longer. OOD composition **fails**.
  - Comparison: OOD succeeds.
  - Model: 8 layers, d=768, AdamW at lr 1e-4, weight decay 0.1, batch 512.

  — [Wang et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.15071)
- Why OOD composition fails: atomic facts are stored in lower layers and a second copy in upper layers for the second hop. Facts never used as a second hop in training have no reason to be copied upward, so they are unavailable to the second hop. — [Wang et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.15071)
- Grokking speed correlates strongly with φ, the ratio of inferred to atomic facts, tested at 3.6–18.0. At φ=18 the model reached 96.7% before training saturated. With φ fixed, varying data size did not change the qualitative behaviour: distribution matters more than size. — [Wang et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.15071)

### Inferences
- **Parametric version.** Suppose the corpus says "x is a c" somewhere and "c can a" somewhere else, and you ask for "x can" with no premises in context. For an unseen x (never seen in any "so x can …" chain), Wang et al. and SCAN "jump" both predict near-chance OOD accuracy, even in much larger models trained far longer. A 50k-parameter model at 2000 epochs should be expected to fail this. That failure is a meaningful, reportable result, not a bug.
- **In-context version (recommended primary task).** Here "x is a c . c can a . so x can" appears within one 32-token window. The model only has to learn a copy-and-bind pattern: find the c after "x is a", find the a after "c can", emit a. This is induction-head-like (Olsson et al.), learnable with at least 2 layers, and immune to the reversal curse because the premises are in context (Berglund et al.). Unseen x then becomes a test of *copying a novel token*, which tiny models can do.
  - Caveat: with BPE, a truly unseen x may be an unseen *token*, whose embedding was never trained. Hold out x as a *trained token that never appears in the reasoning template* (the SCAN "jump"-style test), not as an untrained token.
- **The SCAN dose-response curve is a design dial.** 8, 16 and 32 composed examples gave 38%, 78% and 88%. To have a primitive or entity generalise, it needs several composed exposures. Plan held-out entities to have 0 composed exposures (the OOD test) and in-distribution entities to have at least 8.
- **Mixing ratio (extrapolation from Wang et al.'s φ).** Aim for an inferred-to-atomic ratio of at least about 3–4 in the training corpus: each atomic fact should be *used* in several derived sentences. Include a comparison-type rule ("x is bigger than y") as a control task: Wang et al. predict it generalises OOD where composition does not.

### Gaps
- I found no published SCAN, COGS or two-hop study with transformers under 1M parameters or with a 32-token context. All conclusions about the target model are extrapolations.
- I did not fetch later results showing that tailored data augmentation or architectures improve SCAN/COGS for transformers, so I cannot quantify them here.

## Q5. Expected loss: is the corpus's unigram entropy a meaningful "learned only frequencies" baseline, and is there a standard way to report it?

### Takeaway
Yes. The best possible cross-entropy of any context-free (unigram) predictor equals the unigram Shannon entropy of the token distribution. A model whose loss sits at that level has learned frequencies only. There is no single community-standard table for this, but reporting a ladder of uniform (ln V), unigram entropy, empirical bigram entropy and model loss, in nats per token on both train and held-out text, is well grounded.

### Cited Findings
- A pure unigram model's minimum cross-entropy equals the unigram Shannon entropy. The true per-token entropy rate is strictly lower, and on natural language the two can differ by several bits per token. — [arXiv 2508.15390](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.15390)
- Cross-entropy decomposes into H(X) plus a KL term, so the lower bound for a unigram model is the empirical entropy of the token distribution. — [arXiv 2412.16326](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.16326)
- Cross-entropy is conventionally reported in bits (or nats) per token, with perplexity as its exponential. Bits per token depend on the tokenizer, so comparisons across tokenizers should use bits per byte. — [Edinburgh FNLP slides](https://opencourse.inf.ed.ac.uk/sites/default/files/https/opencourse.inf.ed.ac.uk/fnlp/2023/fnlp06bslides.pdf); [theorempath: perplexity](https://theorempath.com/topics/perplexity-and-language-model-evaluation)
- The unigram model is the standard zero-context comparison point for n-gram or neural models. — [Edinburgh FNLP slides](https://opencourse.inf.ed.ac.uk/sites/default/files/https/opencourse.inf.ed.ac.uk/fnlp/2023/fnlp06bslides.pdf)
- Olsson et al.'s ICL score (loss late in the context minus loss early in the context) is an established way to show that a model uses context beyond frequencies. — [Olsson et al.](https://transformer-circuits.pub/2022/in-context-learning-and-induction-heads/index.html)

### Inferences
- **Concrete reference values for the target vocabulary** (standard arithmetic, not from a source):

  | Vocabulary size | ln V (uniform baseline) |
  |---|---|
  | 100 | 4.61 nats |
  | 200 | 5.30 nats |
  | 400 | 5.99 nats |

  A Zipf-like template corpus will have a unigram entropy well below ln V. Compute the exact value from the corpus as H₁ = −Σ p(w) ln p(w).
- **Recommended reporting ladder**, in nats per token, using the same tokenizer:
  1. Uniform ln V. This is also the expected loss at initialisation.
  2. H₁, the unigram entropy. The model has learned frequencies only.
  3. H₂, the empirical conditional bigram entropy H(wₜ | wₜ₋₁). The model has learned local transitions only.
  4. Model loss on train.
  5. Model loss on held-out sentences.

  Report each as a gap (for example H₁ − loss) and as perplexity. Also report the held-out-reasoning *accuracy* on the conclusion token, which is the real target metric.
- **Tiny-corpus caveat.** On 3–5k tokens with about 50k parameters (more parameters than tokens), the model can memorise the training set and push its train loss far below H₂, or even toward 0. Train loss below unigram therefore does *not* show reasoning. Only held-out loss or accuracy below the bigram baseline, measured specifically on the conclusion token, separates a learned rule from memorisation.
- **Conclusion-token loss.** For a held-out probe "x can ___", compare three quantities: the model's loss on the correct a; the loss of a frequency-only predictor (−ln p_unigram(a)); and the loss of a "majority attribute" predictor. Also report the rate at which the model emits an attribute that *contradicts* the KB (an attribute the KB says x's category lacks) versus one that is merely different. That separates "generalises", "guesses frequent token" and "contradicts the KB".
- Estimate H₁ and H₂ on the *training* split and evaluate them on the held-out split as well. Plug-in entropies on a few thousand tokens are biased low, and on held-out text the unsmoothed bigram cross-entropy can be infinite. Use add-one or similar smoothing and state it.

### Gaps
- I found no paper that standardises a "unigram entropy line" on training curves for tiny transformers specifically. The ladder above is a synthesis.
- I did not fetch work on simplicity bias showing that networks learn n-gram statistics in increasing order (unigram → bigram → …). If confirmed, it would explain why plateaus at H₁ and H₂ are commonly seen early in training, but I did not verify it.

## Synthesis: design rules for the target corpus (all extrapolated to the target scale; sources as cited above)

1. **Size and budget.**
   - Count first: 4k tokens is about 400 sentences of about 10 tokens.
   - With φ (inferred/atomic) ≥ 3 and 2–5 surface forms per fact, this supports about 40–100 atomic facts over about 20–40 entities, 5–10 categories and 5–10 attributes.
   - Grounded in Wang et al. φ; Physics 3.1 multi5; and SCAN's ≥4% coverage giving about 93%.
2. **Repetition versus diversity.**
   - Keep the *reasoning template* rigid: one canonical "x is a c . c can a . so x can a ." frame, the scratchpad analogue (Lee et al.).
   - Vary the *fact statements* in 2–5 paraphrases and orders (Physics 3.1).
   - Rigid reasoning frames make the rule learnable in hundreds of steps. Varied fact frames make facts extractable rather than string-memorised.
3. **Vocabulary.** Use far fewer than TinyStories' 1,500 words. Make every entity, category and attribute a single BPE token if possible, with a small closed set of function words (is, a, can, so, not, .). A BPE vocabulary of 100–400 is adequate.
4. **Step length versus context.** Every premise needed for a conclusion must fall in the same 32-token window as the conclusion. A three-sentence chain at about 5 tokens per sentence is about 15–18 tokens, which leaves room for one distractor sentence. Never let a premise straddle the window boundary. Insert explicit separators between independent "episodes" so that windows sliced from the corpus do not mix unrelated chains.
5. **Mixing reasoning types.**
   - Balance types the way Lee et al. balanced carries.
   - Mix (a) atomic facts, (b) in-context two-hop chains, (c) negative or contradiction chains ("x is a c . c cannot a . so x cannot a"), and (d) a comparison-type rule as a positive OOD control (Wang et al.).
   - Keep each type at roughly equal token share. Ensure every attribute and category appears in at least 8 composed chains (SCAN dose-response).
6. **Hold-outs.** Use three splits (Wang et al. ID/OOD; Physics 3.1 P_train/P_test):
   - **ID-inferred:** chains over entities seen in other chains.
   - **OOD-entity:** entities whose "is a" fact is in training, in several forms, but which never appear in a "so x can" conclusion.
   - **Held-out fact:** an entity with no category stated anywhere. This tests whether the model invents an attribute, and whether that attribute contradicts the KB.

   Evaluate each in two modes: with premises in context (in-context reasoning) and without them (parametric recall). Expect in-context to work and parametric OOD to fail. Never evaluate reverse-direction queries unless that direction is trained.
7. **Optimisation.**
   - The current 300–2000 full-batch Adam steps with no weight decay are in the memorisation regime by grokking standards (Nanda: about 10k epochs at weight decay 1.0).
   - If held-out generalisation is the goal, add weight decay (AdamW, 0.1–1.0) and extend to at least 10^4 epochs. Checkpoint frequently, because transitions are sharp.
   - Use at least 2 layers so induction-style copying is possible.
